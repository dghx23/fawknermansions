An audit-style approach to the book

The book's research applies a standard, textbook evidence-assurance discipline — the same structure used in financial and controls audit work generally, not any specific employer's proprietary methodology:

1. Risk / scope assessment

What's actually knowable about the building, what's unverifiable, and where the biggest gaps in the historical record are likely to be.

2. Evidence gathering

Inspection (archives, records, the building itself), observation (present-day condition and use), and inquiry (residents, heritage bodies, historians) — the three standard evidence-gathering methods.

3. Testing & corroboration

Every claim checked against a primary source where possible; conflicting or unverifiable claims are flagged rather than smoothed over.

4. Reporting

Findings written up so another person could trace any claim back to its source — the same standard applied in §3 Best practice.

3. Best practice for this type of project

Cite everything

Every historical claim traces to a specific archive or record. Don't state anything that can't be sourced.

Keep raw capture data

Archive the unprocessed photogrammetry/LiDAR scans, not just the polished model — that's the part with real long-term preservation value.

Non-invasive capture

No physical alteration, no destructive testing, drone use checked against HO448 and any body-corporate rules first.

Resident privacy

No identifiable images, names, or interviews of current residents published without their explicit written consent.

Separate owner IP from author IP

Agree up front, in writing, what the owners receive unconditionally (scan, archive) versus what remains the author's own work (book, documentary).

Version and date everything

Given the building "might be knocked down," the dated record is the whole point — timestamp every asset.

Give it an institutional home

Lodge copies/records with Stonnington History Centre or a similar body, so the work survives independently of the book's commercial fate.

No invented heritage claims

Stick to what the Heritage Database and archives actually support — inflated claims undermine credibility with the exact institutions worth impressing.

4b. Guest Management & Digital Check-in Coordination

Current state analysis: The building has no guest-coordination layer. Booking engines (Booking.com, etc.) send a generic check-in message. Guests must manually contact management to get a door code and room number. Guest records are hand-written forms (inconsistently filled, filed, or available). No identity verification. No real-time occupancy tracking. No compliance-ready records if authorities need them.

The current Booking.com message — what's working & what's broken

Message received: "We do not have 24 hour reception but we do have a keyless entry system and we ask you to contact us on booking com or by phone to management (Jeff 0401183555) to get the code for the door and your room number. We also ask that you advise us of arrival time so we can assist you with check in... We do have a self check out system where we ask you to simply leave the key in the door of your room on exit."

What's working (the logic) What's broken (the risk)
Keyless entry (door code): Eliminates key pickup friction; guests get immediate access. No accountability: Code is generic or shared; no record of which guest opened which room on which date.
Manual contact (phone/Booking.com): Allows management to communicate directly with the guest. Inconsistent experience: Some get personalized help, others don't. No automation or follow-up. Requires guest to take action.
Self check-out: Reduces labour; guests leave at their own pace. No check-out verification: No record of departure; no damage inspection; no key return accountability.
Arrival time notification: Helps management prepare / know when to expect guests. Optional & unverified: Not all guests provide it. No confirmation system. No occupancy tracking.
Caretaker on-site: Provides emergency oversight and some accountability. No coordination: Caretaker has no digital system to know who's where, when they arrived, when they left, or if they're still on-site.
Budget accommodation positioning: Sets expectations for cost-conscious guests. No guest vetting: No identity verification means potentially problematic guests (criminals, defaulters, etc.) may be accommodated with no record.

The core problems — operational & legal

  • No guest identity verification: Who is actually in the building? Are guests who they claim to be? Is the establishment catering to people it shouldn't be? No photo ID check means no way to know.
  • No occupancy tracking: No real-time view of which rooms are occupied, who's in them, when they arrived/left. Management is essentially blind.
  • No check-in verification: Guest "arrives" but there's no official check-in record, no photo ID capture, no acknowledgement that they received keys or understood house rules.
  • No guest ledger: Hand-written forms (if filled at all) are not filed consistently, not digitized, not available for reference, and not usable if authorities ask "who was staying here on date X?"
  • No check-out accountability: Guest leaves, key goes in door. No inspection of room condition, no damage assessment, no confirmation of departure.
  • Compliance & liability risk: If a serious incident occurs (guest arrested on-site, theft involving a guest, medical emergency, etc.), the establishment cannot produce a consistent, digitized guest ledger. Authorities may have questions the building cannot answer.
  • Bad guest experience: Guests must manually contact management, wait for response, remember a door code, remember their room number. No personalized communication, no confirmation, no clarity.

The better approach — digital check-in coordination layer

Integrate guest management with the WiFi system and booking engine to create a seamless, accountability-rich coordination layer:

Step Current Better (Digital Coordination)
1. Booking confirmation Generic Booking.com message + manual follow-up required Automated personalized email/SMS with check-in instructions, QR link to digital check-in form
2. Pre-arrival verification None (guest is a name on a booking) Guest uploads photo ID during booking or via pre-check-in link; system verifies match (against booking name)
3. Arrival & room assignment Manual phone call; guest told room number verbally; code is generic or shared Guest checks in via digital form (arrives at X time); system auto-assigns room based on availability; generates personalized room-specific door code (valid only for that guest/room/date range)
4. Guest record Paper form (hand-written, filed haphazardly, lost/incomplete) Automated digital ledger: name, ID photo, booking dates, room number, check-in time, check-in signature (digital), house rules acknowledgement
5. WiFi integration N/A Guest WiFi access = verified guest. Guest must authenticate via WiFi to access internet; authentication is tied to their guest record. Provides additional verification layer and real-time occupancy visibility.
6. Check-out Key in door; no inspection, no checkout record Digital checkout form: guest confirms departure time, caretaker/manager does room walkthrough, photos of condition, system records checkout time and signs off
7. Compliance & record Incomplete hand-written forms; no searchable ledger; cannot answer "who was here on date X?" Complete digital ledger: all guest records automatically filed, searchable, exportable, ready for authorities if needed. Audit trail of every check-in/out.

The WiFi link — accountability multiplier

The WiFi system becomes the check-in verification engine:

  • Verified guest = WiFi access: A guest's personalized door code works ONLY if they've completed digital check-in and uploaded ID. No check-in form? No WiFi code generated. No WiFi access without verification.
  • Real-time occupancy: WiFi dashboard shows which room codes are active right now, which guests are currently online, when they connected. Management can see occupancy at a glance.
  • Departure detection: Guest disconnects from WiFi or checks out digitally → room is marked vacant. Management knows instantly when to inspect.
  • Guest contact trail: WiFi portal has guest's phone number/email from booking; if something goes wrong (room damage, missing keys, etc.), management can reach out immediately with proof of who was where.

Critical: Disaster scenario & legal liability risk

In the event of a fire, earthquake, medical emergency, or other disaster, inconsistent guest record-keeping creates catastrophic risks and legal exposure:

  • Accountability failure: If a fire occurs and the building evacuates, emergency responders need an immediate, accurate roster of who was on-site. Hand-written, incomplete forms mean you cannot reliably tell firefighters how many people should have exited or who might still be missing. People may die because the building doesn't know who's where.
  • Regulatory & criminal liability: Fire marshals, building inspectors, and police will investigate. If they find inconsistent or missing guest records, the establishment faces citations for inadequate occupancy tracking — a basic duty of care. Failure to maintain accurate occupancy records during a disaster with fatalities can result in criminal negligence charges against management.
  • Civil liability (families of victims): If someone dies in a fire and the building cannot account for occupants, families may sue for wrongful death under premises liability. The building's inability to produce a verified guest ledger is evidence of negligence — a direct liability multiplier.
  • Due diligence failure: Insurance companies require proof of occupancy record-keeping as a condition of fire/liability coverage. Inconsistent records mean the building may be underinsured or denied coverage — leaving Dr. Nguyen personally liable for damages.
  • Operational/evacuation risk: In an active emergency, caretaker/staff don't know which rooms are occupied, who's checked out, who never arrived. Evacuation sweeps are inefficient and incomplete. People are left behind.
  • Regulatory penalties & operating license: A disaster investigation that reveals inadequate occupancy tracking can result in fines, mandatory remediation, or loss of operating license — ending the business entirely.

This is not hypothetical. A digital guest ledger is a foundational safety and compliance requirement — not an enhancement. It answers the fundamental question "Who is in this building right now?" in a way that hand-written forms categorically cannot. In a disaster, that answer could mean the difference between a successful evacuation and fatalities; between a manageable insurance claim and criminal liability.

Why this matters to Dr. Nguyen: You currently have no visibility into who's in the building, when they arrived, or if they're still here. The paper-based guest ledger is a compliance liability. A digital check-in system isn't a luxury — it's a operational necessity and a risk mitigation essential. The WiFi system becomes the accountability mechanism: no verified check-in, no internet access. Guest experience improves (clear, personalized communication), and the building gains a complete, compliance-ready audit trail that protects you legally.

5. Paid guest WiFi — technical & business case

Not part of the heritage pitch — a distinct, high-margin revenue stream for the owners. The building currently has no guest WiFi, and evidence (from the user's experiment repeating Alfred Hospital's guest network without a password in the hotel foyer) shows substantial organic demand: many guests connected immediately. With proper coverage, payment options, and pricing, this becomes a reliable additional income stream while solving a major service gap and enabling future security infrastructure.

Why this matters

WiFi has become essential. Guests expect it; lack of it is a competitive disadvantage. Beyond guest satisfaction, reliable building-wide connectivity enables:

  • Security infrastructure: CCTV, motion sensors, environmental monitors, and emergency systems requiring reliable network connectivity can finally be deployed reliably.
  • Operational efficiency: Property management, guest services, and staff coordination benefit from consistent network access.
  • Revenue: At ~30 rooms, modest take-up rates generate meaningful cash flow with minimal ongoing labour.

Connectivity model — Starlink backhaul

Technical architecture — OpenWrt + openNDS captive portal

The network topology handles three traffic flows: (1) Starlink WAN → (2) secure building LAN (property management, security systems, resident IoT) → (3) guest WiFi with authenticated, metered access.

Key security attributes:• Guest traffic isolated on 192.168.20.0/24 (br-guest) — no access to building LAN (192.168.1.0/24)• Firewall: guest-to-WAN only; guest-to-LAN blocked explicitly• Client isolation: guests cannot see or communicate with each other's devices• DHCP/DNS: guest devices receive addresses and DNS via gateway at 192.168.20.1• openNDS handles FAS (Forwarding Authentication Service) — real authentication, not just a browser flag

Guest experience & service enhancements

Reliable, high-speed WiFi unlocks services guests now expect as standard:

  • Smart TV streaming services: Guest rooms with smart TVs (Samsung, LG, Sony, etc.) can now reliably connect to Netflix, YouTube, Spotify, and other streaming platforms. This is now the baseline expectation in hospitality — its absence is noticed and negatively impacts reviews and repeat bookings.
  • Guest devices: Smartphones, tablets, laptops can use standard services (email, video calls, banking, shopping) without buffering or dropouts. Work-from-room guests can have video meetings without fear of disconnection.
  • Perceived quality: Reliable connectivity makes the property feel modern, professional, and well-maintained. It's often the first thing guests test and judge.

Security infrastructure & operational visibility

This is the foundational intervention that enables the building's security future:

  • CCTV & camera monitoring: Security cameras currently cannot reliably transmit footage to cloud storage or streaming services due to lack of connectivity. With WiFi, cameras can upload real-time feeds to AWS S3, Dropbox, or a dedicated NVR. Dr. Nguyen can monitor the building from his laptop or phone, anywhere, anytime.
  • Environmental sensors: Temperature, humidity, occupancy, and intrusion sensors can now report to a central dashboard. Alerts can trigger SMS/email if something is amiss.
  • Risk mitigation: From reactive (discovering a problem after the fact) to proactive (spotting issues in real-time, alerting staff immediately). This reduces downtime, theft risk, vandalism, and emergency response time.
  • Peace of mind: Dr. Nguyen has visibility of the building 24/7 without being physically present. This is not just operational — it's psychological reassurance.

Guest-only captive portal, permanent equipment & check-in payment

Guest-only captive portal: the captive portal is the web page a guest sees after joining the guest SSID. It validates a paid/authorised guest entitlement before internet access is opened. It is not used by smart TVs, CCTV cameras, sensors, reception devices or other fixed equipment; those are connected permanently to separate managed networks.

At digital check-in, guests can optionally buy unlimited WiFi for A$6 / 1 night, A$10 / 2 nights, A$13 / 3 nights, A$22 / 7 days, or A$55 / month. The current Lenovo tablet candidate does not provide NFC, so the preferred card-present pattern is a separate integrated EFTPOS terminal (for example Square Terminal via Terminal API or Tyro integrated EFTPOS). Payment success is fed back to the Fawkner backend, which creates a multi-device token tied to the guest, booking and room.

Flow A — bulk self-production

For printing a batch of postcards to keep on hand (events, heritage-group handouts, personal stock). A "Produce" action sends a design + quantity to a print provider and has the batch shipped back to us at cost.

Open print-ready postcards (manual, works today)

NowManual: open the print view, use the browser's print dialog, print or save as PDF, order prints yourself.
LaterA real "Produce" button calling a print-on-demand API — candidates: Officeworks Print & Create (Australia, no public API found — would likely mean a manual upload workflow, not true automation), or a POD service with an actual API such as Prodigi or Printful, which support postcard products and bulk ordering.

Flow B — direct-to-recipient, worldwide

The visitor-facing flow in docs/send-postcard.html: pick a design, write a message, choose "Physical postcard," enter the recipient's address — it's printed and mailed directly to them, wherever they are, without ever passing through us physically.

NowFully built UI (design gallery, delivery-method toggle, address form) — but selecting "Physical" doesn't actually print or post anything. Clearly labelled as such in the flow itself.
LaterCandidate providers with real international print-and-mail APIs: PostGrid and Prodigi both do exactly this (print + direct postal dispatch to an end address, various countries). Neither is integrated — this is a shortlist to evaluate, not a decision.
Data implicationA real integration means collecting and (briefly) storing a full postal address per postcard sent — worth deciding a retention/deletion policy before this goes live for real, not after.

Making postcards free — funding options

Provider sponsorship

Approach the eventual print/mail provider directly — they cover print+postage cost in exchange for a small branding credit on the postcard back or a case-study/PR mention. Providers running POD services often have exactly this kind of co-marketing budget.

Heritage/interest-group funding

Same funding conversation as the book (see §7 and docs/project-brief.md's public-interest section) — Stonnington Council, National Trust, or a university partner could plausibly fund a batch of postcards as part of a wider heritage-promotion grant.

Self-funded as marketing spend

The postcard itself promotes the book, documentary, and building — so its cost can reasonably be treated as marketing/PR spend rather than pure overhead, especially if it drives book sales or press attention (see §2's legacy/media argument).
